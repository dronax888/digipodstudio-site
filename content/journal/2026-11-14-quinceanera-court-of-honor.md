After the date, the venue and the dress, one of the first big decisions in quinceañera planning is the court of honor. These are the friends and family who walk in with her, dance the waltz beside her and stand next to her in most of the photos. Choosing them can feel exciting and a little stressful at the same time, for her and for the parents.

This guide explains what the court does, how many people to ask, how to choose them and how to ask in a way that keeps everyone happy, from the first rehearsal to the last dance.

## What is a quinceañera court of honor?

The court of honor, or *corte de honor*, is the group of young people who accompany the quinceañera through her celebration. The girls are called **damas** and the boys **chambelanes**. Together they usually:

- Walk in with her at the mass or ceremony and at the reception entrance.
- Learn and perform the choreographed waltz, and sometimes a surprise dance.
- Sit near her at the reception and help keep the party moving.
- Take part in the formal photos before and after the event.

One chambelán has a special place: the **chambelán de honor**. He is her main escort and dance partner for the day. He often walks in beside her, dances the waltz with her and may join her in other moments of the program, such as her entrance or a toast.

## How many damas and chambelanes should she have?

There is no fixed number. A traditional court is often described as **14 couples plus the quinceañera and her chambelán de honor**, so that together they represent her fifteen years. Many families still love that symbolism.

Today, most courts are smaller and built around what works for the family. Common choices include:

- **A large court** of seven couples or more, for a big formal celebration with a full choreography.
- **A small court** of three to six couples, which is easier to rehearse and coordinate.
- **Only a chambelán de honor**, or only a few chambelanes, which keeps the focus on her.
- **Only damas**, or a mixed group of close friends, for a more relaxed party.

When you decide, think about the size of the dance floor, the budget for outfits and accessories, how many people you can realistically bring to rehearsals and, most of all, who she truly wants beside her. A court of four people who show up to every practice will look better on the dance floor than a court of twenty where half are missing.

## Who to choose (and who to think twice about)

Most quinceañeras pick from the same few groups: close friends from school, cousins, siblings and friends from church, a sports team or a dance class. A few questions help narrow the list:

1. **Will they commit to rehearsals?** The waltz usually takes several weeks of practice. Ask about work, sports seasons and other activities before you invite someone.
2. **Do they get along with each other?** The court spends a lot of time together. A group that already likes each other makes rehearsals fun instead of tense.
3. **Can their families support it?** In many families each court member covers some of their own costs, such as the dress or suit rental and shoes. Be clear about this early so nobody feels caught off guard.
4. **Is the chambelán de honor someone she trusts?** He will be in many of her most important moments and photos. Many girls choose a close friend, a cousin or a brother rather than a crush, so there is no awkwardness if things change before the party.

It is also fine to keep the court small and honor other important people in different ways, such as reading at the mass, giving a toast or being thanked by name in the program.

## How to ask them

Asking is part of the fun. Many quinceañeras now turn it into a small moment of its own:

- **A short video message** sent to each friend, with her photo and the question "Will you be my dama?" or "Will you be my chambelán?"
- **A small gift box** with a note, a sweet treat or a little crown charm.
- **A group dinner or sleepover** where she asks everyone at once.

Whatever she chooses, include the key facts with the question: the party date, the rehearsal plan and what the family is asking each person to cover. A clear ask now avoids misunderstandings later.

Give everyone a few days to talk to their parents before they say yes. If someone says no, thank them warmly and move on.

## Planning rehearsals

The waltz is the moment the court is remembered for, so rehearsals deserve a real plan.

- **Start early.** Many families begin practicing two to three months before the party, or earlier for longer choreographies.
- **Set a regular schedule.** One or two practices a week at the same time and place makes it easier for everyone to commit.
- **Consider a choreographer.** Many families hire a choreographer to teach the waltz and the surprise dance. Some keep it simple and have a relative or dance-class friend lead the practices.
- **Share the details in one place.** A group chat or a party website with the rehearsal calendar, the address and any changes saves dozens of separate messages.
- **Plan a final run-through** at or near the venue so the court knows the entrance, the floor and where to stand.

## Outfits and colors

The court's outfits usually follow the party's color theme. Damas often wear matching or coordinated dresses in a shade that complements the quinceañera's gown without matching it exactly, so she still stands out. Chambelanes often wear suits, tuxedos or a traditional *charro* outfit, with a tie, vest or flower in the theme color.

Decide the colors before you ask the court, so families know what they are agreeing to. If you are still choosing, our guide to [quinceañera color themes](@/journal/quinceanera-color-themes/) has palettes that work for the gown, the court and the invitations.

## Thanking the court

The court gives a lot of time and energy to the celebration. A few simple ways to say thank you:

- **Thank them by name** during the toast or in the program.
- **A small gift** for each dama and chambelán, such as a keepsake, a framed photo or a personal note.
- **Include them in the slideshow.** A few photos of the court at rehearsals or getting ready make a sweet section in her photo slideshow, and the court loves seeing themselves on the big screen. ## Frequently asked questions

### How many people are in a quinceañera court?

There is no fixed number. A traditional court is often 14 couples plus the quinceañera and her chambelán de honor, representing her fifteen years. Many families now choose a smaller court of three to six couples, or only a chambelán de honor, depending on space, budget and who can commit to rehearsals.

### What does the chambelán de honor do?

He is the quinceañera's main escort for the day. He usually walks in with her, dances the waltz as her partner and stands beside her in many photos and special moments of the program.

### Who pays for the court's outfits?

It depends on the family. In many celebrations each dama and chambelán covers some of their own costs, such as the dress or suit, while the hosting family may pay for accessories or the choreographer. Whatever you decide, explain it clearly when you invite each person.

### When should she choose her court?

Many families choose the court about a year before the party, after the date and venue are booked. That leaves time for everyone to plan and for rehearsals to begin a few months before the celebration.
